Scannell and Gifford's (2010) tripartite person-process-place framework of place attachment offers a useful lens on these findings, particularly its explicit borrowing from Bowlby's (1982) attachment constructs - proximity, safe haven, secure base, and separation distress - originally developed to describe interpersonal rather than person-place bonds. Several of this project's findings map closely onto these constructs. Portable rituals such as Sian's travel teabags or Karolina's habit of adding plants to a space function as safe-haven behaviours: small, repeatable acts that recreate felt security independent of location. Ellie's explicit distinction between a practical, administrative ‘base’ and a felt sense of home maps closely onto the distinction between a secure base (a stable point of return that enables exploration) and the affective experience of attachment itself - the two can coexist without fully overlapping, exactly as Ellie describes.

Mallett's (2004) critical review of the home literature notes that some researchers understand people as feeling at home in the world by ‘grounding their experience less in a place and more in the activities that occur in this place’ - a framing that fits this project's Activity-coded word responses (Erika's dancing, Emma's dance party, Shila's description of a place to be both chill and creatively intense) more precisely than either the Feeling or Place categories alone.
