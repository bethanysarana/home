The regularity with which difficult personal history surfaced in this dataset raises a genuine interpretive question: is this a property of home as a topic, or a property of this specific method? Asking someone to narrate their understanding of home appears to function, in practice, as an invitation into autobiographical reflection more broadly - consistent with home's status in the literature as what Mallett (2004) describes as a concept entangled with self, family, and journeying, rather than a narrowly bounded topic. Several participants' accounts also carry the hallmarks of what the post-traumatic growth literature (Tedeschi & Calhoun) describes as active, positive meaning-making following adversity - travel explicitly framed as recovery, reflection, or a deliberate second attempt at something difficult, rather than only escape. This project does not attempt to diagnose or formally assess this in any individual account, and any resemblance to that literature is offered as an interpretive possibility rather than a claim about any specific participant's psychological state.
