Giddens' (1991) concept of ontological security, and its application specifically to home by Dupuis and Thorns (1998), adds a complementary explanation for why settling-in behaviours matter as much as they appear to in this dataset. For Giddens, routines and a continuous, coherent biographical narrative are not incidental to psychological stability but constitutive of it. This offers a plausible mechanism for Michel's finding that a known departure date suppresses home-making behaviour: if ontological security depends on sustaining a sense of narrative continuity, a pre-planned exit interrupts that continuity project before it begins, reducing the incentive to invest in place-based routine. The same logic extends to Eduardo's case - his repeated refusal to name anywhere home can be read as a deliberate withholding of narrative continuity, protecting against a second experience of its loss.

Cohen, Duncan and Thulemark's (2015) concept of lifestyle mobility - characterised by ‘the intention to move on, rather than move back’ - offers a precise description of this sample, distinguishing participants from both tourists (who intend to return) and conventional migrants (who intend to resettle permanently). This has a direct bearing on how the headline finding of this project should be interpreted: a population defined partly by having already chosen not to be fixed to one place is, almost by definition, more likely to describe home in portable, relational terms than a general population would be. This is addressed further as a limitation in Section 4.6, but is worth noting here as an interpretive frame in its own right, not only a caveat.
