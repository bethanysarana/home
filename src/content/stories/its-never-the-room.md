For several women in the sample, the deciding factor was physical safety rather than comfort: Erika left a travelling group whose drinking culture felt coercive; Smila describes a solo stay made unbearable by being stared at and photographed without consent; Paulina left Sumatra early over a similar concern.

The most theoretically useful case may be one that did not register as a threat to belonging at all. Bella describes a day of real physical hardship in Sydney, treated by her and the interviewer alike as endurance rather than evidence the place did not suit her.

> *“I'm going to survive this, but Lord, I'm being tested right now.” — Bella, on a day of real physical hardship in Sydney that both she and the interviewer treated as endurance rather than a threat to belonging*
>
> *“It's about acknowledging that your time is limited and you can't be in two places at once.” — Bella, reframing homesickness as an awareness of finite time with specific people rather than distance from a place*


Bella's fuller account adds a related, quieter case: an initial inability to understand fellow students' homesickness during study abroad, which she attributed at the time to having nothing pulling her backward. Homesickness caught up with her later anyway, once she had people worth missing, and she now frames it as unconnected to place entirely.

Read together, these cases suggest the relevant variable is not the presence of hardship but its source: hardship that is purely logistical or physical does not appear to threaten belonging, while hardship that is social, relational, or safety-related consistently does - even when comparatively minor.
