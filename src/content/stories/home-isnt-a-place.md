Across the sample, when participants were asked to distil their sense of home into a single word or short phrase, the large majority produced a feeling, a named person or people, or a shared activity, rather than a description of a building or location.

> *“Home... is very much an emotion and something which you feel deep within you... it doesn't have to necessarily be a place or a person, but just, it could be a sound, a taste, a smell.” — Sammy, offered entirely unprompted, before any question about the concept of home had been asked.*
>
> *“Home is a feeling that you carry with you.” — Karolina*
>
> *“I'm not coming back in a house. I'm coming back to a home and my home is my people, my family.” — Guilia, whose one-word answer was not an abstract noun but her sister's first name.*
>
> *A bright orange lily that bloomed from unknown wildflower seeds outside her university house, on the day everyone moved out, already wilting by evening. — Bella's shape for home: a combination of nature and people, fragile precisely because it depends on both.*

Eduardo's account is the clearest exception, and it resists a single tidy reading. His one-word answer, ‘a beach in Venezuela’, is the only literal, geographic answer in the dataset, and his is the only account organised entirely around the loss of an original home.

> *“The home that I call home... it's not there anymore, it doesn't exist.” — Eduardo*
