Childhood home structure was coded for 20 of 26 participants, revealing three configurations alongside the modal single, stable home: two contrasting homes held simultaneously, usually through separated parents; chronic residential mobility; and a secondary, non-primary property recalled ahead of the actual primary residence.

> *Parents' house versus grandmother's house, held at once. — Anna*
>
> *‘Home home’ versus a stepfather's weekend lake bungalow. — Miri*
>
> *Mother's apartment versus father and stepmother's household, alternating by custody. — Cloe*

Ellie's account is the clearest case of chronic mobility: five countries and approximately fifteen houses across childhood. She distinguishes a practical, administrative sense of home from a felt sense of home, and is explicit the second does not override the first. Bella sits at the other end of this spectrum, one house from age six with both parents present, a useful reminder that the sample's dominant relational finding does not depend on childhood instability to produce it.

> *“I would never be like, oh, the mountain in Japan... home was our flat.” — Ellie, whose childhood spanned five countries and roughly fifteen houses*


A related but distinct pattern appears in adulthood rather than childhood: Arturo names his family home in Madrid as a safety net (‘if everything goes wrong, I have my place there’) while separately calling Gran Canaria, where he has chosen to live, his actual home - holding both labels without treating them as contradictory.
